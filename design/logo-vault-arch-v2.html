Vault Arch — V2 Brief

Nasser rejected all three V1 variations. This is the postmortem and the enriched brief for three dramatically more sophisticated directions.

Postmortem — V1 Rejections

What Nasser Rejected, and Why It Failed

Three variations were submitted in logo-vault-arch-variations.html. Nasser reviewed all three and rejected every one. The verdicts were blunt: "too basic", "not good enough", "awful". Before creating V2, we must understand exactly why each failed — not to repeat the same mistakes.

Variation 1 — The Single Line Verdict: "Too basic"

One SVG path with a stroke. That's it — M28 88 L28 46 A22 22 0 0 1 72 46 L72 88 with a 3px gold stroke and a threshold line. No architectural detail, no presence, no craft. It reads as a generic archway doodle, not a luxury brand mark. Cartier-level restraint requires Cartier-level confidence — a single line only works when the proportion, weight, and context are so precise that the line alone communicates heritage. This didn't earn that confidence.

Variation 2 — The Vault Door Verdict: "Not good enough"

Right direction, weak execution. Three nested concentric arcs (r=28, 20, 12) with a faint 14% Vermeil gradient fill and a center seam. The concept of layered depth was sound — but the result looked like three overlapping curves, not a vault door. No voussoirs, no keystone, no architectural detail that would make it read as a real vault entrance. The gradient at 14% opacity was nearly invisible. The center seam was a line, not a feature. "Better but not good enough" means the direction is viable — the execution must be dramatically richer.

Variation 3 — The Arch & T Verdict: "Awful"

Bad combination from the start. The italic-T monogram already exists as the brand's app-icon mark (§1.2). Forcing it inside an arch frame created visual conflict — two symbols competing for the same space, neither enhanced by the other. The arch became a cage around the T rather than an independent architectural statement. Do not combine the arch with the monogram. The arch must stand on its own as an architectural mark. This direction is retired.

Key Learnings

What V2 Must Do Differently

Three principles distilled from the rejections. Every V2 variation must satisfy all three.

Principle 01 — Detail earns presence

  • A single stroke is too minimal. Luxury marks have craft: architectural elements, geometric precision, considered line weights.
  • Think of the keystone, the voussoirs, the moldings — the elements that make a real arch feel built, not drawn.
  • The mark must look like something a master craftsman designed, not a quick SVG path.

Principle 02 — Layering needs substance

  • Nested curves alone are not enough. V1 Variation 2 proved this.
  • Depth must come from real architectural or geometric logic — stone courses, inlay channels, interlocking planes — not just stacking arcs.
  • If using fill or gradient, it must be visible and intentional, not a 14% whisper that disappears.

Principle 03 — The arch stands alone

  • Never combine with the T monogram. V1 Variation 3 was "awful" — two marks fighting.
  • The arch is an architectural mark, not a frame for something else.
  • The Trésor name appears as the wordmark beside or below the mark, never inside it.

The bar — real luxury quality

  • Cartier — the panther, the geometric C, the restraint of perfect proportion
  • Hermès — the carriage, the ironwork detail, the heritage seal
  • Tiffany & Co. — the oval, the line weight, the negative space
  • Bulgari — the Roman letterforms, the geometric strength
  • Van Cleef & Arpels, Asprey, Boucheron — the heraldic crest, the Art Deco geometry
  • If it looks like a generic SVG shape, it has failed. Research first, then design.

Three New Directions

The Vault Arch, Reimagined

Three distinct registers. Each must be immediately distinguishable from the others and from every rejected V1 variation.

A
Variation A

The Architectural Vault

A detailed vault arch entrance — the kind you'd see on a real private bank vault or a heritage treasury building. This is not a line drawing of an arch; it is an architectural elevation with the structural elements that make a real arch: a keystone at the crown, voussoirs (the wedge-shaped arch stones) radiating from the keystone down to the springing points, imposts (the horizontal moldings where the arch begins), and jamb blocks forming the vertical door frame. The interior reveals depth — a receding threshold suggesting the vault interior beyond. This is the entrance to something permanent and valuable.

Must include

  • Keystone — a distinct trapezoidal stone at the arch's crown, wider at top than bottom (the wedge that locks the arch)
  • Voussoirs — 7 to 9 wedge-shaped stones radiating from keystone to springing points, separated by hairline mortar joints
  • Impost moldings — a horizontal band at the springing line (where arch curves begin), the structural transition from jamb to arch
  • Jamb blocks — vertical stone courses on left and right, suggesting the door frame's mass
  • Receding threshold — 2–3 steps of depth inside the arch opening, suggesting the vault interior
  • Sill/base line — a ground line anchoring the structure

Must avoid

  • Single-line arch — this is exactly what V1 V1 was
  • Three nested curves — this is what V1 V2 was
  • Any letterform or monogram inside the arch
  • Excessive ornamentation — architectural detail, not decorative filigree
  • Mortar joints so fine they disappear at 32px
  • Uneven voussoir spacing — study real Roman/Syrian arches for proportion

B
Variation B

The Geometric Inlay

The arch formed from intersecting geometric shapes with a gold inlay effect — inspired by Art Deco luxury (Cartier's geometric panther, Boucheron's architectural珠宝, the Chrysler Building's crown). The arch is not drawn as a curve but constructed from overlapping planes: a semicircle, intersecting rectangles, a stepped profile. The gold inlay creates depth through two tones of gold — bright gold (#E8D5A3) and deep gold (#9A7E4A) — filling alternating segments like a marquetry panel. Negative space is not empty; it is active geometry. The mark should feel like it could be engraved on a vault door face.

Must include

  • Arch form built from geometry — semicircle + rectangles + stepped profiles, not a single arc stroke
  • Gold inlay with two tones — #E8D5A3 (bright) and #9A7E4A (deep) filling distinct segments, creating a marquetry/lacquer effect
  • Negative space as design element — the void inside the arch is shaped, not just "empty"
  • Stepped or faceted profile — Art Deco rhythm, not organic curves
  • Hairline structure lines — thin gold lines defining the geometric framework, visible at 128px
  • Symmetry — left-right mirror, the mark must feel balanced and deliberate

Must avoid

  • A plain semicircle with a stroke — that's V1 V1
  • Concentric arcs with faint fill — that's V1 V2
  • Art Deco pastiche — inspired by, not imitating. No sunbursts, no zigzags, no generic Deco ornament
  • Gradient fills that look like CSS — the inlay should look like metal marquetry, not a smooth gradient
  • More than 2 gold tones — restraint is luxury
  • Letterforms inside the geometry

C
Variation C

The Heraldic Crest

The arch as the central element of a heraldic crest — a coat-of-arms-style mark with heritage weight. The arch sits within a shield or cartouche outline, flanked by laurel branches or a ribbon banner bearing the Trésor name. This is the most traditional of the three, drawing from Asprey's heritage crests, Hermès' equestrian seal, and the tradition of European house marks. The arch is not alone — it is enshrined. The overall form should feel like something stamped on a wax seal or engraved above a treasury door. Permanent, institutional, aristocratic.

Must include

  • The arch as the central charge (heraldic term for the main element) — architectural, not minimal
  • Shield or cartouche outline — a rounded, symmetric frame around the arch, suggesting a crest
  • Laurel branches OR a ribbon banner — one heraldic flourish, not both. Laurel = classical heritage; ribbon = institutional.
  • TRÉSOR wordmark set in Playfair Display 500, uppercase, letterspaced — either on the ribbon banner or below the crest
  • Hairline construction throughout — no heavy fills, no gradients. The luxury is in the line work.
  • Symmetry and balance — left-right mirror, weight distributed evenly

Must avoid

  • The arch alone with no heraldic context — that's V1 V1/V2
  • Overwrought Victorian ornament — heraldic, not baroque. Restraint within the tradition.
  • Both laurel AND ribbon — pick one. Luxury means knowing when to stop.
  • Monogram T inside the crest — retired direction, do not combine
  • Shield shapes that look like generic logos — study real heraldic shields
  • Fill colors beyond the gold/ink system

Shared Requirements

For Every Variation

Technical

  • Pure inline SVG — no external assets, no JS, no PNG fallbacks
  • Both colorways: gold #C9A961 on charcoal #1A1715 AND ink #1A1715 on cream #FAF7F2
  • Both sizes: 128px (full detail) and 32px (simplified proof)
  • ViewBox 0 0 100 100 on all marks
  • Each SVG has role="img" and descriptive aria-label

Typography

  • Playfair Display — the wordmark, headlines, editorial accents
  • Jost — labels, kickers, body, construction notes
  • Wordmark: TRÉSOR in Playfair Display 500, uppercase, letterspaced
  • The É accent is always gold (#E8D5A3 on dark, #9A7E4A on cream) — per §1.1
  • No emoji, ever

Quality bar

  • Research real luxury logos first — Cartier, Hermès, Tiffany, Bulgari, Van Cleef & Arpels, Asprey, Boucheron
  • Study their geometry, negative space, proportion, line weight
  • Each variation must be visually distinct from the other two AND from all three rejected V1 variations
  • If any variation looks like "a curve with a stroke," it has failed
  • The HTML itself is a design document — match the Trésor aesthetic
